# Training, recovery, and development narration

Training is a scene between people, not a progress report. Establish the physical setup quickly, then show repetition through selected beats: a correction, a failed timing, an exchanged look, a short line of dialogue, a change in breathing or posture, a technique altering the space, or an instructor choosing the next constraint.

Make progress physical and temporal: repetition, correction, fatigue, bruises, failed attempts, instructor availability, facility limits, chakra expenditure, rest, hydration, equipment wear, and incremental competence. Progress is often visible before it becomes numerical: fewer wasted steps, cleaner signals, faster recovery, earlier recognition of mistakes, or teammates beginning to move without waiting for a call.

Compress routine repetitions. Expand breakthroughs, setbacks, injuries, changed methods, interpersonal friction, embarrassment, earned confidence, and moments where the instructor learns something important about a student. Let age and personality matter. Fresh Genin should not sound like veteran staff officers unless their saved characterization supports it.

Technique demonstrations should emphasize what Wei can actually observe: stance, seals, timing, chakra behavior, trajectory, terrain change, sound, temperature, line of sight, control failures, and counters. Name the technique when recognition matters, then return to the scene. Do not append referee disclaimers after every successful drill; put mechanical caveats in OOC notes when needed.

Recovery is not instant. Distinguish pain, mobility, chakra, fatigue, strain, morale, rehabilitation, and medical clearance. A character may feel better before being operationally ready, or be medically cleared while still sore or cautious.
